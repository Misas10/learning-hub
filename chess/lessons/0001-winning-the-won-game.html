Chess · Lesson 1

Winning the Won Game

You win a knight on move 18. Twenty moves later you've lost. Nothing about your chess knowledge failed — you just played the wrong kind of chess for the position you were in.

Why this, first? You said you get winning positions and don't convert them. Those are the cheapest rating points available: the hard part — getting ahead — is already done. This lesson gives you one rule and two questions to use from the moment you go up a piece.

The root fact

An extra piece is worth more the fewer pieces are left.

Seven pieces against six is a small edge, and the other six can still make threats. One piece against none is total control. Every fair trade moves you from the first situation toward the second. Chess.com's own reference puts it flatly: "Most of the time, when you are ahead in material you should proceed with even exchanges."1

Everything else in this lesson follows from that one fact.

Consequence 1: trade pieces

If trades grow your advantage, make them. Queens first — the queen is the piece most able to cause you trouble. National Master RobRam, writing for chess.com: "trade pieces of the same value. I would do this even if I'm only up a pawn."2

And don't be fussy about the price. Dan Heisman's example: "if you are ahead a knight and can force the trade of queens but it will double your pawns, double away."3

Consequence 2: but keep your pawns

Follow the trades to the end. You have a king and a knight; they have a king. That is a draw — a lone knight or bishop cannot force checkmate. The win comes from a pawn that your extra piece escorts to promotion. No pawns, no win.

So the rule has a second half, and it's the half people forget. Heisman words it carefully: "Make fair trades of pieces, not necessarily pawns."3 Note that a piece means a knight, bishop, rook or queen. Pawns are not pieces.

Flip the board and you get the defender's rule for free: "When ahead pieces trade pieces, when behind pieces, trade pawns."4

Consequence 3: their only hope is chaos

If calm trades win for you, the opponent's only chance is that something wild happens. Heisman: "Complications always favor the player who is losing."3 So Heisman's first principle when ahead is "Think Defense First" — not passive play, just looking at their threats before your own ideas.

RobRam turns this into two questions to ask every move once you're ahead:2

  1. "How can I ruin this?"
  2. "What's the easiest way to convert?"

Recall before you check

Start from the root fact and rebuild the lesson. What follows from "an extra piece is worth more the fewer pieces are left"? Trades grow the advantage → so make fair trades of pieces, queens first, even at a small structural cost → but the last step of the win is a pawn promoting, so keep pawns on → the defender wants the reverse (keep pieces, swap pawns) and their only other hope is complications → so look at their threats before your ideas: "How can I ruin this?", then "What's the easiest way to convert?"
Why is "trade everything" the wrong summary of this lesson? Because pawns are not pieces. Trade everything and you reach king and knight against king, which is a draw. The third position above is exactly that trap.

Take it to the board

The skill only counts in a real game. In your next five rapid games:

  1. The move you go up a piece or more, say the two questions in your head.
  2. After the game, note one line: was I ahead? did I win? if not, which move gave it back?
  3. Bring those notes to the next session. They decide what Lesson 2 is.
